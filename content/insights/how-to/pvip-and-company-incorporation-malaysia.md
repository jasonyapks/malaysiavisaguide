## Why founders look at Malaysia

Malaysia sits in the middle of ASEAN, a short flight from Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. Its legal system comes from English common law, its politics are stable, and its infrastructure and trade policy are built for companies that want to grow across the region.

The numbers shown in the webinar point the same way:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Total approved investment | RM426.7 billion, up 11% year on year |
| Annual GDP growth | 5.7% |
| New jobs created | More than 240,000 |
| SME sector growth | 5.7% |

Day to day, the practical advantage is language. English is the working language of business, contracts and most government paperwork, so you can negotiate, register a company and deal with regulators without waiting on translations.

Life outside the office matters too. Malaysia costs a fraction of Singapore or Hong Kong to live in, its private hospitals are good enough to draw medical tourists from across the region, and there is a wide choice of international schools.

What makes all of that usable is long-term residency. With the right visa, you can sit as a director of your own company, skip the work-permit route and run the business yourself.

## What PVIP gives you

The Malaysian government introduced the Premium Visa Programme (PVIP) in 2022 for business owners, high-net-worth investors and senior executives. Most residence schemes let you live in the country but stop you working there. PVIP was designed to close that gap: it gives you the right to live in Malaysia, invest, and run a Malaysian company directly.

- **Up to 20 years.** A multiple-entry visa issued as four renewable five-year passes.
- **Work and run a business.** You can work, invest, own and manage a Malaysian company without a separate Employment Pass or work permit.
- **No minimum stay.** You can travel as much as you like without putting your residency at risk.
- **No minimum age** for the principal applicant.
- **Family on one application.** Your spouse, parents and dependent children below 25 can be included. A child who reaches 25 comes off your visa and needs a status of their own.

MYPVIP is a licensed agent appointed by the Immigration Department of Malaysia. It reports a 100% approval record across more than 250 family applications from over 30 nationalities. Its five largest applicant groups come from China, Taiwan, Japan, the United States and the United Kingdom.

## Who qualifies

There are three requirements, and none of them is complicated to understand.

**Offshore income** of more than RM40,000 a month (about USD 9,500), or RM480,000 a year (about USD 115,000). You show it with three to twelve months of payslips, investment statements, dividend vouchers or rental records.

If you live somewhere without personal income tax, such as the UAE, you won't have tax returns to submit. You can instead give the Immigration Department a formal declaration that you don't file locally, backed by bank statements that show the money arriving.

**A fixed deposit** of RM1,000,000 (about USD 240,000) with a licensed Malaysian commercial bank, placed after approval.

**A clean police record**, shown by a letter of good conduct or police clearance certificate from your home country or country of residence.

## How the fixed deposit works

Once the full deposit has been in place for six months after your visa is endorsed, you can withdraw up to RM500,000 for approved spending in Malaysia: buying property, medical costs, or school fees for your children.

Property bought with that withdrawal still has to meet each state's minimum price for foreign buyers, for example RM1,000,000 in Kuala Lumpur and RM2,000,000 in Selangor. If you bought property in Malaysia before your visa was endorsed, it may still count; submit proof of title and it will be assessed.

Interest is paid every year. You can roll it into the deposit or have it paid into a personal Malaysian savings account.

On tax, Malaysia works on a territorial basis, so foreign-sourced income earned offshore is not taxed in Malaysia. That exemption has been extended to 2036. You become a Malaysian tax resident by spending at least 183 days in the country in a calendar year.

## The application, stage by stage

1. **Documents and submission.** About a month to prepare, then around three months with Immigration. The file covers certified passport pages, police clearance, proof of income, bank statements, employment or business ownership records, tax documents and family certificates. Your licensed agent submits it to the Immigration Department.
2. **Your visit to Malaysia, one to two weeks.** After approval you fly in, open a personal bank account, place the RM1,000,000 deposit, have a standard medical check and take out private medical insurance. Insurance is compulsory if you are 60 or under. If you have a pre-existing condition, the policy can be arranged to still meet Immigration's requirements.
3. **Incorporation, three to seven working days**, through the Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM).
4. **Opening for business.** Municipal permits, a corporate bank account, hiring, and growing into the region.

Getting PVIP before you incorporate is worth the wait, because it shapes who can hold shares and sit on the board. Sort the visa first and you avoid the governance problems most foreign founders run into.

## Choosing your company structure

The structure you pick decides how flexible you can be, how you're taxed, how much paperwork you carry and how much risk you take on.

| Structure | What it is | Foreign ownership | How it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sendirian Berhad (Sdn Bhd) | A private limited company, legally separate from its shareholders | Up to 100% in most sectors | The usual recommendation. It can sign contracts, trade, issue tax invoices, hire local and foreign staff, and borrow from banks. |
| Branch office | A direct extension of the foreign parent company | Follows the parent's ownership | Higher risk: the parent carries all the liability, and Malaysian banks, suppliers and customers prefer dealing with a local Sdn Bhd. |
| Representative office | A liaison presence with no legal personality and no trading | Wholly owned by the parent | Tightly limited to market research and customer liaison. It cannot invoice, sign sales contracts or earn revenue. |

### Which sectors are open

You can own 100% of a company in general trading, management consulting, technology, software, import and export, and corporate services.

Retail and wholesale distribution, construction, private education, financial services, telecommunications, and oil and gas are different. They come with equity limits, local-shareholding conditions or their own licences.

Once foreign shareholders hold 25% or more of a company, it counts as foreign-controlled, which changes how some municipal and ministry approvals are handled.

### Agriculture is a special case

Agritech and commercial farming have real growth potential, but foreign ownership in agriculture is closely controlled.

- Foreign firms often need a local or Bumiputera equity partner before they can get operating permits.
- Most states restrict foreign ownership of agricultural land, so access usually comes through a lease or a joint venture.
- Licensing goes through the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security, and the paid-up capital required is higher.

### Get your business activity codes right

When you register with SSM, you choose objective codes that describe what the company does. Get them wrong and it costs you later.

Datin Jiniy Seelan of JS Partners described a founder who registered a Sdn Bhd under general trading codes. The business actually ran e-commerce fulfilment out of a warehouse. When it applied for its premises licence and digital trading permits, the local council turned both down because the codes didn't match the activity. Fixing it meant amending the constitution, refiling with SSM and losing more than three months.

So before you incorporate, make sure the codes cover what you will really do: trading, warehousing and anything you sell online.

## Incorporating a Sdn Bhd

Incorporation is done online through SSM's MyCoID system and usually takes three to seven working days.

Under the Companies Act 2016, every Sdn Bhd needs four things:

1. **A registered office** in Malaysia, usually your company secretary's address, where legal notices and government letters are served.
2. **A company secretary**, licensed by SSM or a member of a prescribed professional body, appointed within 30 days of incorporation to look after compliance.
3. **Paid-up capital**, with a legal minimum of RM1. That changes if you want to sponsor foreign staff on Employment Passes: then it's RM1,000,000 for a wholly foreign-owned company, RM500,000 for a joint venture and RM250,000 for a wholly Malaysian-owned one.
4. **A resident director** whose main or only home is in Malaysia.

### The resident director problem, and how PVIP solves it

The resident director rule has always been the biggest hurdle for foreign founders. The usual workaround is to appoint a local nominee director, and that comes with real risks.

- **You can lose control of the bank account.** Banks often want a local director's signature, and in a dispute that can freeze the business.
- **The nominee holds real legal power.** They have a director's statutory powers, which exposes you operationally and legally.
- **It costs money every year** in nominee fees.

A PVIP holder doesn't need any of this. PVIP gives you long-term legal residence in Malaysia with no minimum stay, so you can be your own resident director. You keep full control of the company and sign for the bank account yourself.

If the company has to be set up before your PVIP is endorsed, a corporate secretarial firm can put a professional nominee in place as a short-term bridge. The directorship passes back to you as soon as your visa is issued.

### The four steps

1. **Name search and reservation, one to two working days.** You check and reserve the company name on the SSM portal.
2. **Documents.** The constitution is drafted (optional under the Companies Act 2016, but worth having if there is more than one shareholder), the objective codes are chosen, and each director's statutory declaration is prepared.
3. **Signing and filing.** Shareholders and directors sign electronically and the file goes to SSM. You don't need to be in Malaysia for this.
4. **Registration, one to three working days.** SSM issues the Notice of Registration under Section 15, and the company legally exists.

## After incorporation

A few registrations have to be in place before you invoice anyone or take on staff.

### Tax

SMEs pay a lower rate on their first slice of profit:

| Chargeable income | Rate |
|---|---|
| First RM600,000 | 15% to 17% (SME tiers) |
| Above RM600,000 | 24% (standard rate) |

Register with the Inland Revenue Board (LHDN) within three months of incorporating to get your tax file number and avoid penalties. Service tax (SST) registration becomes compulsory once taxable turnover reaches RM500,000 in any 12-month period.

If you set up inside the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone (JS-SEZ), there are extra tax incentives, faster customs processing and logistics arrangements built for supply chains that run between Singapore and Malaysia.

### Premises licences

If you take an office, shop or warehouse, you'll need a premises licence and a signboard permit from the local council, which takes about one to two weeks. Regulated businesses, such as private healthcare, education and some specialised retail, also need a permit from their ministry at the same time.

### The corporate bank account

Banks check new accounts closely under anti-money-laundering rules. Expect to provide:

- the SSM Notice of Registration and the company constitution
- a certified board resolution authorising the account
- directors' passport copies and proof of address
- a detailed business plan
- verified customer or supplier invoices
- proof of where the capital came from

A standard corporate account needs an opening deposit of RM1,000 to RM5,000. The most common reasons for delay are vague business descriptions and capital that can't be traced, so clear, verified paperwork is what gets you through quickly.

### Hiring: PVIP compared with the Employment Pass

New rules from 1 June 2026 made the Employment Pass much harder to get for foreign staff and foreign directors, roughly doubling the salary thresholds.

| Category | Monthly salary | Longest pass | Knowledge-transfer plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Category I | RM20,000 and above | Up to 10 years | Not required |
| Category II | RM10,000 to RM20,000 | Up to 10 years | Required, with an audited local succession plan |
| Category III | RM5,000 to RM10,000 | 5 years at most | Required, with an audited local succession plan |

For Categories II and III, the employer has to document and run a formal programme to train Malaysian staff, and every renewal depends on showing that the skills were actually passed on.

Timing is the other problem. Immigration quotes three to six months for an Employment Pass, but with several agencies involved in some sectors, it can take up to a year in practice.

PVIP avoids all of this. You aren't subject to the higher salary thresholds, the succession paperwork, the RM1,000,000 paid-up capital rule for sponsoring foreign hires, or the long wait.

### Payroll registrations

Within 30 days of hiring your first employee, the company must register with:

- the **Employees Provident Fund (EPF)**, the compulsory retirement scheme for local staff
- the **Social Security Organisation (SOCSO)** and the **Employment Insurance System (EIS)**, which cover workplace injury and unemployment

### The yearly compliance calendar

Your company secretary keeps track of the filings that keep the company in good standing:

- **Annual return** to SSM, within 30 days of each anniversary of incorporation.
- **Audited financial statements**, prepared by an external auditor, presented to shareholders and then filed with SSM and LHDN.
- **Corporate tax return** to LHDN, within seven months of the financial year-end.

## Putting it together

Pairing 20-year PVIP residency with a Malaysian Sdn Bhd gives you a sound base for doing business across ASEAN. You get the legal, operational and financial rights to run the company yourself, without a minimum stay, so you keep control of the board while managing your tax position and getting to market quickly.

In short, you get:

- up to 20 years of multiple-entry residency, with no requirement to stay in Malaysia
- the right to be your own resident director, with no nominee and no separate work permit
- SME tax rates of 15% to 17% on your first chargeable income, and the foreign-sourced income exemption to 2036
- freedom from the higher Employment Pass salary thresholds, succession paperwork and capital rules
- a company registered online in three to seven working days

## Where to start

1. **Check your immigration readiness.** Book a session with a licensed PVIP adviser to review your offshore income documents, police clearance and how your family file should be put together.
2. **Plan the company and tax side.** Work with a company secretary and tax adviser, such as JS Partners, to choose the right SSM objective codes, confirm which licences you need and decide on paid-up capital.
3. **Do it in order.** Get PVIP approval, place the fixed deposit, incorporate, open the corporate bank account, and start trading in Malaysia and across the region.
