You've asked three firms what it costs to open a company in Malaysia and got three versions of the same answer: "from RM1,500". It's true, and it's almost useless. The registration is the cheapest line on the bill, and the firms quoting it know that.

Here's the part nobody writes down. For a foreign owner, the number that decides everything usually isn't a fee at all. It's the RM500,000 of capital the company has to hold before it can sponsor your own work pass. If you need that pass, the question isn't whether you can afford RM1,500. If you don't need it, you may be able to run the whole thing for less than a month's rent in Mont Kiara.

**The short answer:** registering a Malaysian private company costs about RM1,600 to RM3,100, including the RM1,000 government fee. A foreign owner's real first-year cost runs from roughly RM5,000 if you already live in Malaysia on a pass that allows business, to RM15,000–26,000 if you need a resident director, plus RM500,000 or more in capital if the company must sponsor your work pass.

## Which founder are you?

Three kinds of foreigner set up a Malaysian company. They pay very different amounts for the same certificate, so find yourself first.

**You'll stay abroad.** You want a Malaysian company to invoice regional clients, hold a contract or test the market, and you're not moving. You don't need a visa. You do need someone who lives in Malaysia on your board, and that is your biggest cost.

**You're moving here to run it.** You want the company to employ you, which means it has to sponsor your Employment Pass. That brings in the capital requirement, and for the first months, before you're resident, a resident director as well.

**You already live here.** You hold a pass in your own right that allows business activity. You can be your own resident director, and the company doesn't have to sponsor anybody. This is the cheapest position by a wide margin, and most people in it don't realise how much it saves them.

## What each of you pays in year one

Two things stand out. The incorporation line is identical in every column, and for anyone who needs a resident director it's a small fraction of the bill. And the gap between the columns comes from one question: does a director already live in Malaysia?

## The resident director rule

SSM's rule is short. A private company needs at least one director who ordinarily resides in Malaysia by having a principal place of residence here. It says nothing about citizenship. A foreigner who genuinely lives in Malaysia satisfies it. A Malaysian who lives in London doesn't.

If you live abroad, you have two options. A business partner or trusted contact in Malaysia can take the seat. Or you pay a provider for a nominee director, typically about RM3,000 to set up and about RM600 a month, usually with a minimum term and a refundable deposit of around RM5,000.

Be clear-eyed about what a nominee is. That person is a real director under Malaysian law, with real duties and real signing power, and they're taking on personal liability for a company they don't control. That's why it costs what it does, and why a reputable provider will want the deposit, limits on the bank mandate and a look at what the business actually does. A nominee who asks no questions is not the bargain it looks like.

If you're moving here, you need the nominee only until you're resident yourself. Budget for six months, then replace them.

## Paid-up capital: the number that isn't a fee

To register a company, capital is a formality. The figures that frighten people come from somewhere else: the Immigration Department's Expatriate Services Division, which a company must register with before it can hire any foreigner, including its owner.

| Who owns the company | Paid-up capital ESD asks for |
| --- | --- |
| 100% Malaysian-owned | RM250,000 |
| Joint venture with foreign equity | RM350,000 |
| 100% foreign-owned | RM500,000 |
| Foreign-owned, in wholesale, retail and trade | RM1,000,000 |

Paid-up capital is your money, in your company's account, and the company can spend it on running the business. But it has to be there, it has to be real, and you can't quietly take it back out later. If you're the founder who is moving here, this is your true cost of entry. Plan around RM500,000, not RM1,500.

## The costs nobody quotes

**Service tax on everything.** Professional fees carry 8% service tax. Add it to every service line in the table.

**The trading licence.** If your foreign-owned company will sell goods, import and distribute, or run a shop, café or restaurant, it falls under the Ministry of Domestic Trade's rules on foreign participation in distributive trade. That means ministry approval and RM1,000,000 of paid-up capital before you open, whichever pass you hold. The ministry has said these guidelines are under review, so check the current version before you sign a lease.

**The tax rate you don't get.** Small Malaysian companies pay 15% and 17% on their first RM600,000 of profit. A company that is more than 20% foreign-owned doesn't qualify and pays 24% from the first ringgit. We explain that test in [our guide to Malaysian tax for expats](/insights/expat-living/malaysian-tax-for-expats/).

**Audit.** A private company can now skip the audit if it meets two of three size tests on revenue, assets and staff, with the revenue test at RM2,000,000 for financial years starting in 2026 and RM3,000,000 from 2027. Ask your company secretary to confirm you qualify. If you don't, expect about RM1,200 to RM2,500 for a small company. Exempt or not, you still file accounts and a tax return every year.

**Annual filings.** Some firms bundle the annual return and the lodging of your accounts into the secretary's retainer. Others charge them separately, and that adds roughly RM500 to RM1,000 a year from the second year. Ask which you're being quoted before you compare two monthly fees.

**Changes after the fact.** Appointing or removing a director, moving the registered address, transferring shares and raising capital are each charged separately on a basic secretarial plan, typically RM250 to RM750 a time. A founder who replaces a nominee and injects capital in the first year will use several of them.

## The order it happens in

1. **Reserve the name and incorporate.** SSM charges RM50 to reserve a name and RM1,000 to incorporate. A clean application is usually approved within a few working days.
2. **Appoint a company secretary** within 30 days of incorporation. In practice the firm that incorporates you does this the same day.
3. **Open the bank account.** This is the slow step for foreign owners. Banks want to understand who the directors are and where the money comes from, and many want to meet a director in person. Allow weeks, not days.
4. **Pay in the capital** and have the secretary record the share allotment.
5. **Get your licences.** A local council premises licence for most physical businesses, and the trade ministry's approval if you're in distributive trade.
6. **Register with ESD and apply for your pass,** if the company is sponsoring you. Only now does the Employment Pass application begin.

A founder who needs the pass should think of the whole sequence in months.

## A company is not a visa

This is the misunderstanding that costs people the most. Owning a Malaysian company gives you no right to live in Malaysia, and no right to work in your own business. You get those from a pass, and there are two ways to hold one.

The company can sponsor you for an Employment Pass, which is what the capital requirement above is for. The lowest tier needs a salary of at least {{employment-pass:salaryFloor:money}} a month, and the pass ties your right to stay to that one company. If the business closes, the pass goes with it.

## So which is yours?

If you're **staying abroad**, budget RM15,000 to RM26,000 for the first year plus a refundable deposit, and spend your effort choosing the resident director, not the cheapest incorporation.

If you're **moving here to run it**, your real number is RM500,000 of capital, or RM1,000,000 if you'll trade, sell or serve food. The fees are a rounding error next to it. Decide whether the business justifies that before you register anything.

If you **already live here** on a pass that allows business, you're looking at RM5,000 to RM16,000 and no capital hurdle, unless you're going into distributive trade. You're the one person for whom "from RM1,500" is nearly honest.

And if you're still choosing a pass, choose it with the business in mind. The cheapest company in Malaysia belongs to the founder who sorted out their own right to be here first.
