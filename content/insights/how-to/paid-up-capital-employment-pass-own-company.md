The company was the easy part. It took a week and cost less than a flight home. Then you asked how it sponsors your work pass, and someone mentioned a number with five zeros that nobody had brought up before.

That isn't an accident. The firm that incorporates you is selling incorporation. The capital requirement belongs to a different office in a different building, and it only matters to the kind of founder who wants to live here. You've probably worked out by now that nobody in the chain had a reason to tell you first.

**The short answer:** a 100% foreign-owned Malaysian company needs RM500,000 of paid-up capital before the Immigration Department will let it employ a foreigner, including its owner. It's RM1,000,000 if the company is in wholesale, retail or trade. The money is yours and the company can spend it, but it has to be real, and it can't be quietly withdrawn afterwards.

## Where the number comes from

Not from company law. SSM will register a company with a single share.

It comes from the Expatriate Services Division (ESD) of the Immigration Department. Before a company can apply for an Employment Pass for anyone, it must register with ESD, and ESD sets a minimum paid-up capital by who owns the company.

| Who owns the company | Minimum paid-up capital |
| --- | --- |
| 100% Malaysian-owned | RM250,000 |
| Joint venture with foreign equity | RM350,000 |
| 100% foreign-owned | RM500,000 |
| Foreign-owned, in wholesale, retail and trade | RM1,000,000, plus the trade ministry's licence |

MIDA, which processes Employment Passes for manufacturing and selected services companies, publishes the same three figures in its June 2026 guideline.

## Which tier is yours?

**You own all of it.** RM500,000. This is most foreign founders.

**You have a Malaysian co-owner.** RM350,000. A saving of RM150,000, in exchange for a real partner holding real shares. Don't invent a partner to get it; we explain why in [can a foreigner own 100% of a Malaysian company?](/insights/how-to/foreigner-100-percent-ownership-sdn-bhd/)

**You'll trade, sell or serve food.** RM1,000,000, and you need the Ministry of Domestic Trade's approval before ESD will look at you. That ministry's own rule is RM1,000,000 in shareholders' funds per outlet.

## What paid-up capital actually is

It's the price you pay your own company for its shares. You transfer the money into the company's bank account, the company issues shares to you, and the company secretary files the allotment with SSM.

After that, it's the company's working money. It can pay the rent, the staff and the suppliers with it. Nothing in the rule says the balance has to sit untouched.

Three things it is not:

- **It isn't a fee.** Nobody keeps it.
- **It isn't a government deposit.** It isn't lodged with Immigration or frozen in a fixed deposit.
- **It isn't yours to take back on a whim.** Returning capital to a shareholder means a formal capital reduction or closing the company. Paying it out to yourself as a "loan" the month after approval is the kind of thing that invites questions at renewal.

So the real question isn't whether you have RM500,000. It's whether you have RM500,000 you're prepared to commit to this business.

## The salary comes with it

The pass has its own floor. Under the salary policy in force since 1 June 2026, Category I needs at least RM20,000 a month, Category II at least RM10,000, and Category III at least {{employment-pass:salaryFloor:money}}.

Your company has to actually pay you that, every month, and you'll pay Malaysian income tax on it. A founder planning to draw nothing for the first year can't do that on an Employment Pass. Budget for the salary and the tax as well as the capital.

## The order, and the gap in the middle

1. **Incorporate.** You'll need a director who lives in Malaysia from day one. You don't live here yet, so this is usually a nominee for the first months.
2. **Open the bank account.** Slow for foreign-owned companies. Allow weeks.
3. **Pay in the capital** and file the share allotment.
4. **Get any sector licence** the business needs.
5. **Register the company with ESD.**
6. **Apply for your Employment Pass.**
7. **Replace the nominee** with yourself once you're resident.

Notice the gap. Between steps 1 and 6 you own a funded Malaysian company and have no right to work in it. Plan your travel, and what you do while you're here, around that.

## Is it worth RM500,000?

This is where you decide which founder you are.

**You're building an operating business.** You'll have premises, staff, stock or equipment. The capital is money you'd have to put in anyway, and the Employment Pass is the natural route. The requirement costs you very little that the business wasn't going to cost you already.

**You're a consultant with a laptop.** Your business needs RM20,000, not RM500,000. Parking half a million in a company so that it can employ you is an expensive way to get a pass that ends the day the company does. Look at a pass you hold in your own right first.

**You want to live here and keep options open.** The Premium Visa Programme asks for a fixed deposit of {{pvip:fixedDeposit:money}} and a participation fee of {{pvip:participationFee.principal:money}}, and its work rights are recorded in our guide as: {{pvip:workRights:text}}. That's more money than the capital requirement. But the pass is yours for {{pvip:tenureYears:years}}, it doesn't depend on any company surviving, and a PVIP holder who lives here can be their own resident director. Compare them properly in [which Malaysian visa lets you run a business?](/insights/comparisons/which-visa-lets-you-run-a-business/)

The Employment Pass is the cheaper door. It's also the one that closes behind your company. If the business fails, so does your right to stay, and that's worth knowing before you choose it for the price.
