Somebody has told you that you need a Malaysian partner. It might have been an agent, a friend who set up here in 2005, or a local businessman who would be happy to hold 51% for you. They all said it with complete confidence.

For most businesses it isn't true, and it hasn't been for years. What you've noticed, and probably haven't seen written down, is that the people who say it most firmly are often the ones who'd benefit from being the partner.

**The short answer:** a foreigner can own 100% of a Malaysian private company in most sectors. The Companies Act sets no limit on foreign shareholding. What the law does require is one director who lives in Malaysia. Separate licensing rules apply in a few sectors, most often retail, trading and restaurants, where a foreign-owned company needs ministry approval and RM1,000,000 in capital.

## Two rules people confuse

Almost every bad decision here comes from mixing up two different requirements.

**The ownership rule** asks who holds the shares. For a Malaysian company in general, there isn't one. MIDA, the government's investment authority, puts it plainly: the Companies Act 2016 does not stipulate any equity conditions on Malaysian-incorporated companies. Conditions come only from the ministry or agency that licenses a particular activity.

**The residence rule** asks where a director lives. SSM requires every private company to have at least one director who ordinarily resides in Malaysia by having a principal place of residence here. It says nothing about citizenship and nothing about shares.

So "you need a local" is half right. You need a local resident on the board. You don't need a local owner. Those are very different things to give someone.

## Which situation are you in?

**Your business is open to 100% foreign ownership.** Consulting, software, design, marketing, most professional and business services, and manufacturing fall here for most founders. You can hold every share yourself. Your only structural question is the resident director.

**Your business sells goods or food.** If you'll wholesale, retail, import and distribute, or run a café or restaurant, you're in distributive trade and a different rulebook applies. You can still own 100%, but it costs more.

**Your business is in a regulated industry.** Banking, insurance, telecommunications, private education, healthcare, logistics, oil and gas and a few others are licensed by their own regulators, some of which do cap foreign equity. Ask the regulator, not an incorporation agent.

## If you sell goods or food

This is the rule that catches people after they've signed a lease.

Since 2010, all foreign involvement in distributive trade needs the approval of the Ministry of Domestic Trade. Under the ministry's guidelines, as MIDA sets them out:

- The business must be **incorporated locally** as a Malaysian company. A branch of a foreign company doesn't qualify.
- It must hold **at least RM1,000,000 in shareholders' funds**, meaning paid-up capital plus reserves, **for each outlet**.
- Opening, moving or expanding outlets each needs approval again.

"Specialty stores" in these guidelines include restaurants and food catering outside hotels, food and beverage, household goods, furniture, electrical goods, healthcare products and footwear. Larger formats such as hypermarkets, superstores and department stores face heavier conditions still.

Two cautions. The ministry has said it is reviewing these guidelines, so read the current version before you commit. And the capital is per outlet: a second café is a second million.

This is the honest reason some foreigners take a local majority partner. It's a way around the RM1,000,000. Whether it's worth half your company is the question to ask before you agree, not after.

## The resident director, and what a nominee really is

If you live in Malaysia, you can be the resident director yourself. Nationality isn't the test, residence is. Company secretaries in practice accept a foreigner who lives here on an Employment Pass or the Premium Visa Programme as meeting it.

If you live abroad, someone else has to take the seat. That can be a business partner or employee in Malaysia, or a paid nominee director. A nominee typically costs about RM3,000 to set up and about RM600 a month, with a refundable deposit of around RM5,000. We break down the full first-year bill in [what it costs to set up a company in Malaysia](/insights/how-to/company-setup-cost-malaysia-foreigner/).

Understand what you're buying. A nominee director is a real director. They carry the legal duties of the office and personal liability for a company they don't control, which is why a careful one will ask what the business does, limit their own signing authority and want that deposit. You still own the shares and you can remove them.

## The arrangement that actually goes wrong

A nominee **shareholder** is a different thing entirely, and it's the one to be afraid of.

Here, a Malaysian holds your shares in their own name, usually to make a foreign-owned trading business look local. On the register, they own the company. Your ownership exists only in a private trust deed. If the relationship sours, or they die, divorce or go bankrupt, you're the one who has to go to court to prove the company is yours.

It also doesn't hide what people think it hides. Malaysian companies must now identify their beneficial owners, defined as the natural persons who ultimately own or control the company, whether through shares or by other means, and keep them in a register. SSM's guidance is explicit that someone can be a beneficial owner while holding less than 20% of the shares, or none. The arrangement is visible to the regulator, and it leaves you with the weaker legal position.

If the only reason for a local shareholder is to avoid a licence, you're trading a known cost for an unknown risk.

## So which is yours?

If your business is **open to full foreign ownership**, own all of it. Sort out the resident director, either by living here or by paying for a nominee, and don't give equity to anyone for a seat a service can fill.

If you're in **retail, trading or food**, decide between two honest options: fund the RM1,000,000 and own it outright, or bring in a Malaysian partner who is a real partner, with money and work in the business, on terms a lawyer has written.

If you're in a **regulated industry**, your first conversation is with the regulator.

And if your plan is to live in Malaysia and run the company yourself, the director question and the visa question are the same question. A company doesn't give you the right to live here; a pass does. Start with [the PVIP guide](/visas/pvip/) or [the Employment Pass guide](/visas/employment-pass/).
