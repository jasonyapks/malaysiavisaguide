Everyone who's moved to Kuala Lumpur has a story about how cheap the doctor was. A specialist the same week, a scan the same day, a bill a fraction of what it would have been at home. It's true, and it's the reason a lot of people choose Malaysia in the first place.

What those stories leave out is that they're usually told by people under 60 with an employer's insurance card in their wallet. Once you're paying for your own cover, at an age when insurers start to hesitate, the cheap doctor's bill stops being the number that matters. **The premium is.** And for a foreigner, there are two more things the stories don't mention: a tax on private treatment that Malaysians don't pay, and public hospitals that charge you full cost.

**The short answer:** private treatment in Kuala Lumpur is reasonably priced, with a GP visit capped at RM80 and a hospital screening package from about RM330, but a non-citizen pays 6% service tax on top. Public hospitals charge foreigners RM120 to see a specialist, against RM5 for a citizen. Whether to insure depends mostly on your age and your visa.

## Two systems, two price lists

Malaysia runs a public system, heavily subsidised for citizens, and a large private system that most foreigners use.

**Public hospitals charge foreigners a separate, published rate.** These are the Ministry of Health's charges for foreigners:

| Public hospital service | Foreigner (RM) |
| --- | --- |
| General outpatient visit | 40 |
| Specialist outpatient visit | 120 |
| Emergency department | 100 |
| Ward, third class, per day | 160 |
| Ward, second class, per day | 180 |
| Ward, first class, single room, per day | 320 |
| Intensive care, adult, per day | 360 |
[^1]: Ministry of Health, Caj Bagi Warga Asing, page last updated 23 September 2025. These are the charges for the visit or the bed. Investigations, procedures and medicines are charged on top. A citizen pays RM1 for a general visit and RM5 for a specialist, as the Health Minister confirmed in June 2026.

Those numbers aren't high by international standards, and public hospitals in Kuala Lumpur include the country's main referral centres. But you'll wait, you'll be asked for a deposit before admission, and you won't choose your doctor. Most foreigners who can afford private care use it.

**Private hospitals and clinics charge everyone the same price, and then add 6% if you're not a citizen.** Since 1 July 2025, private healthcare given to non-citizens carries 6% service tax. The Customs Department's FAQ is explicit on two points: Malaysian citizens are exempt, and no category of non-citizen is. Holding MM2H, PVIP or an Employment Pass doesn't change it.

What private care costs, before the 6%:

- **A GP visit:** the consultation fee for private clinics was revised in April 2026 to a range of RM10 to RM80, the first change since 2006. Medicines and tests are charged separately.
- **A health screening:** Pantai Hospital Kuala Lumpur sells a doctor-led package from RM329 and a specialist-led one for RM680. Gleneagles Hospital Kuala Lumpur lists heart screening at RM1,975 for men and RM2,189 for women.
- **An admission:** this is where bills grow, and there's no published price list for it. A hospital will usually ask for a deposit at admission and give you an estimate. Ask for the estimate in writing, and ask whether it includes the 6%.

## What your visa makes you buy

Before you decide anything, check what your pass already requires:

- **MM2H:** medical insurance is required for the main applicant and each dependant, and it's priced by the insurer, not the government. Applicants aged 60 and over are exempt. That's MYPVIP practice as at July 2026, and MOTAC's May 2026 booklet likewise asks for health insurance at renewal only "if below 60 years old".
- **Sarawak MM2H:** local medical insurance for the main applicant under 60, and for every dependant at any age.
- **PVIP:** health insurance held for the whole life of the pass.

So if you're under 60 on any of these, insurance isn't a choice. The decision only opens up for an MM2H or Sarawak MM2H holder over 60, which is the group with the most to lose from getting it wrong.

## Why premiums are the real risk

Medical insurance premiums in Malaysia rose sharply in 2024, enough for Bank Negara Malaysia to step in. Its interim measures, which run until the end of 2026, make insurers spread increases caused by medical inflation over at least three years. By the central bank's own account, the increase for more than 90% of repriced policies was below 10%. Policyholders aged 60 and over on a minimum plan got a one-year pause.

Read that carefully, because two things in it matter to an older foreigner. **The measures cover increases from medical inflation, not increases because you've moved into an older age band.** Age-band increases were excluded. And **the measures end in 2026.** Nobody has said what replaces them.

A new basic plan, MediAsas, is due in January 2027. Everything published about it so far describes it for Malaysians, and we found nothing saying whether a non-citizen can buy it. Don't build a retirement plan around it until the terms are out.

## Which choice is yours?

**You're under 60, on MM2H, Sarawak MM2H or PVIP.** You're insuring anyway, so buy well. Get cover that pays the hospital directly, check the annual and lifetime limits against private-hospital prices in Kuala Lumpur, and check what happens to your premium at 60, 65 and 70. A policy that's cheap at 55 and unaffordable at 68 is a trap you can see from here.

**You're over 60 on MM2H and healthy.** You have three options, and each has a cost:

1. **Insure locally while you still can.** Premiums rise with age bands, and the older you are when you apply, the more an insurer will exclude. If you're going to insure, sooner is cheaper.
2. **Keep or buy an international plan.** It may cost more, but it can follow you if you leave Malaysia. Compare how it reprices with age before you assume it's safer.
3. **Self-insure.** Set aside a hospital fund and use public hospitals for anything major. At published foreigner rates, a week in a first-class single room is RM2,240 for the bed, before treatment. A serious illness at a private hospital, with 6% on top, can cost many times that. This works only if the fund is real money you won't touch.

**You're a family on an Employment Pass.** If your employer provides group cover, check whether it includes your spouse and children, and read what it actually covers before you turn down a private top-up, especially for maternity and anything pre-existing.

**You're on DE Rantau.** You're probably young, healthy and here for a year or two. An international nomad policy is often simpler than a Malaysian one, but check it covers treatment in Malaysia, not only emergency evacuation.

## Three questions to ask before you sign anything

1. **Does the quote include the 6% service tax?** It applies to every private bill you run up as a non-citizen. An insurer's limits are in ringgit, so the tax eats into them.
2. **What does the premium become at the next two age bands?** Ask for it in writing. That number is your real cost.
3. **Can the insurer reprice or decline renewal because of claims?** A policy that drops you after your first serious claim isn't insurance.
