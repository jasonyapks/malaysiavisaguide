The first month in Kuala Lumpur, most people can't believe how little they're spending. A plate of noodles from a hawker stall, a taxi across town, a bag of vegetables from the market: it all costs a fraction of what it would at home.

By the third month the bills look different, and it's rarely the rent. It's the supermarket that stocks the brands you know, the bottle of wine on a Friday night, and the car, because you're paying for the petrol at a price your Malaysian neighbour doesn't. **Kuala Lumpur is cheap for the way Kuala Lumpur lives.** The further your habits are from that, the more you'll pay.

**The short answer:** everyday local food is cheap and officially tracked: about RM9 for a kilo of chicken and RM15 for 30 eggs in October 2026. A foreigner pays more than double a Malaysian's price for petrol (RM4.32 a litre against RM1.99) and three times as much for an unlimited transit pass. Alcohol carries heavy excise duty, raised by 10% in November 2025.

## How Kuala Lumpur actually spends

Here's something that surprises most newcomers. The average Kuala Lumpur household spends more on **eating out** than on **food at home**: RM1,346 a month against RM976, according to the Department of Statistics' 2024 survey. Eating out is the city's second-largest spending line after housing.

That tells you how the city works. Hawker centres, kopitiams and food courts are where Kuala Lumpur eats, and the prices there are set by local competition, not by what expats will pay. If you eat that way, your food bill can be lower than at home. If you cook at home with imported ingredients, it won't be.

## Local food: what it costs, officially

The trade ministry publishes prices recorded at shops and markets across Malaysia under its PriceCatcher programme. These are the medians recorded in Kuala Lumpur between 10 and 23 October 2026:

| Item | Median price (RM) | Shops recorded |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chicken, standard, 1 kg | 9.39 | 120 |
| Eggs, grade A, 30 | 15.30 | 130 |
| Tomatoes, 1 kg | 6.20 | 161 |
| Mackerel (ikan kembung), 1 kg | 18.90 | 65 |
| Fresh milk, 700 g | 9.40 | 76 |
| Imported white rice, 10 kg | 34.99 | 15 |
| Imported buffalo meat (India), topside, 1 kg | 24.79 | 29 |
[^1]: KPDN PriceCatcher, prices recorded 10 to 23 October 2026 at premises in W.P. Kuala Lumpur, median of all recorded prices. Computed from the open data on 23 October 2026.

You pay exactly what a Malaysian pays for all of these. There's no foreigner price at the market. What PriceCatcher doesn't track is the imported cheese, the Australian beef, the breakfast cereal you grew up with. Those are sold mostly in premium supermarkets in the expat areas, at prices that reflect shipping, import duty and the shop's rent in Mont Kiara or Bangsar. No official series measures them, so the only honest advice is to walk through one before you set your food budget.

## Alcohol: where the budget really goes

If you drink, this is the line that will surprise you. Malaysia taxes alcohol heavily, and Budget 2026 raised the excise duty on alcoholic drinks by **10% from 1 November 2025**, the first increase since 2016. Beer is now taxed at **RM192.50 per litre of pure alcohol**.

On a 330ml can of 5% beer, that's about **RM3.18 in excise duty alone**, before import duty, sales tax and anyone's margin. Wine and spirits are taxed on the same principle at their own rates. It's why a bottle of supermarket wine that would be cheap at home is a considered purchase in Kuala Lumpur, and why a round of drinks in a bar can cost more than dinner.

For comparison, the average Kuala Lumpur household spends RM96 a month on alcohol and tobacco together. Most of the city doesn't drink much, so nothing about the market is built to make it cheap.

## Getting around: the two prices

This is where being a foreigner costs you directly, because transport subsidies are tied to the MyKad, the Malaysian identity card.

**Petrol.** Since the BUDI95 subsidy started, Malaysians buy RON95 at a fixed RM1.99 a litre, up to a monthly quota, verified with their MyKad at the pump. Everyone else pays the unsubsidised price, set weekly by the Ministry of Finance. In the week of 22 to 28 October 2026 that was **RM4.32**. RON97, which isn't subsidised, was RM4.80 for everyone.

Put that into a month. If you drive 1,000 km a month in a car that uses eight litres per 100 km, you'll buy about 80 litres. At RM4.32 that's about RM346, against RM159 for your Malaysian neighbour driving the same car the same distance.

**Public transport.** The **My50** pass, RM50 for 30 days of unlimited rail and bus rides in the Klang Valley, is for Malaysians only. The pass open to foreigners, **Rapid Bulanan**, costs RM150 for 30 days of unlimited travel on Rapid KL's LRT, MRT, BRT, monorail and buses. The tourist MyCity pass was withdrawn on 1 January 2026. You can still pay single fares instead.

**Owning a car.** A foreigner can buy and register a car in Malaysia. Car-buying guides say dealers and lenders generally want to see a long-term pass, and that a loan is harder to get than it is for a citizen. Factor in the petrol price above, parking in the expat areas and tolls on the expressways, and set it against ride-hailing, which many foreigners in central Kuala Lumpur use instead of owning a car at all.

## Which way of living is yours?

**You'll live local.** Eat at hawker centres and food courts, shop at the wet market, take the train with a Rapid Bulanan pass and use ride-hailing for the rest. This is where Kuala Lumpur's reputation for being cheap comes from, and it's real. Your everyday spending can sit well under the city average.

**You'll import your lifestyle.** You'll cook with imported ingredients, drink wine with dinner and drive your own car. Budget for it honestly, because every one of those choices meets a tax or a price that local life doesn't.

**Most people end up in between.** They eat out locally most days, keep imported food for the weekend and drink less than they did at home, sometimes because of the prices. If you can live like that, the cost-of-living numbers you've read about are within reach.
