Some time in your first week, someone will ask you "Can or not?" and wait for an answer. A colleague will say they're going to tapau lunch, and your Grab driver will describe the traffic as "very teruk one". Every word of it counts as English, and you won't find any of it in a phrasebook.

This is Manglish, or Malaysian English. It draws on English, Malay, Hokkien, Cantonese and Tamil, and Malaysians of every background use it, including the bank manager once they're off the clock. The rest of this guide covers the parts you'll hear most.

## The little words that do all the work

Much of Manglish's tone sits in short particles at the end of a sentence. They do the job punctuation can't, and once you can tell them apart, a lot of conversations start to make sense.

{% table caption="The essential particles" %}
| Particle | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **lah** | Softens or stresses, depending on tone | "Don't worry lah." · "Come lah!" |
| **lor** | Resigned acceptance | "No more seats, so we stand lor." |
| **meh** | Doubt or surprise | "Got such thing meh?" |
| **mah** | "It's obvious, isn't it?" | "I told you already mah." |
| **leh** | A gentle push or a mild complaint | "Help me a bit leh." |
| **one** | Adds emphasis to a description | "This durian very nice one." |

There's no point memorising rules for these, because nobody here follows any. Listen to the tone instead. Within a month or so you'll hear the difference.

## Phrases you'll hear every day

**Can or not?** means "Is it possible?" or "Will you?" The answer is usually "can" (yes), "cannot" (no) or, if they're keen, "can can".

**Got or not?** asks whether there's any, or whether you have it. "Got" stands in for "have" and "is there" almost everywhere: "Got parking or not?"

**Where got?** means "No way, that's not true." Tell a friend "You so rich!" and expect "Where got!"

**Already** marks something as done, so "I eat already" means "I've eaten."

**Is it?** (often "izzit") is a reaction, roughly "Really?", rather than a question about anything in particular.

**Then how?** means "So what do we do now?"

**Send** means drop someone off. "I send you to the airport" is a kind offer, not a shipping arrangement.

**On the light** and **off the light** mean switch it on and switch it off. Give it a year and you'll be saying them too.

**Outstation** means out of town: "Boss is outstation this week."

**Gostan** means reverse the car. It comes from the old nautical "go astern", and it's what the parking attendant will be shouting at you.

## Food words

Eating is close to a national hobby, so ordering food is where a bit of Manglish pays off fastest.

**Makan** means eat, or food. "Jom makan!" ("Let's eat!") is the most useful sentence in this guide.

**Tapau** means takeaway. It's Cantonese in origin, but everyone uses it. **Bungkus** is the Malay word and means the same thing.

**Mamak** restaurants are the 24-hour Indian-Muslim places that work as the country's shared living room. Try one at midnight over a roti canai.

**Kurang manis** means less sweet. Drinks here come sweet by default, so you'll use this a lot.

**Boss** is what you call whoever takes your order. It doesn't imply any hierarchy.

### Ordering at the coffee shop

Kopitiam drink orders have a grammar of their own. Start with the base drink and add the modifiers you want:

{% table caption="Kopitiam drink codes" %}
| Order | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| **Kopi / Teh** | Coffee / tea with condensed milk (sweet) |
| **Kopi O / Teh O** | Black, with sugar |
| **Kopi C / Teh C** | With evaporated milk and sugar |
| **…kosong** | No sugar |
| **…peng / …ais** | Iced |
| **Teh tarik** | "Pulled" milk tea, poured from a height until it froths |

So "kopi O kosong ais" gets you iced black coffee with no sugar. Say it without hesitating and the uncle behind the counter will take you for a regular.

## How to address people

Malaysians rarely call strangers by their first names. Family terms do that job instead.

**Uncle** and **auntie** work for any older man or woman: the hawker, the neighbour, the security guard. They're polite and warm.

**Abang** and **kakak** are Malay for "big brother" and "big sister". Use them for someone around your age or a little older. In speech they shorten to **bang** and **kak**.

**Adik** is for someone younger. Waiters often get "dik".

**Boss** covers anyone serving you, as above.

## Feelings and reactions

**Alamak!** is "Oh no!", for spilled teh tarik, missed exits and forgotten wallets.

**Aiyoh** and **aiyah** cover exasperation, mild or deep.

**Walao (eh)** is Hokkien for disbelief, and you'll mostly hear it in traffic.

**Paiseh**, also Hokkien, means embarrassed or sorry to trouble you: "Paiseh, can I sit here?"

**Kiasu** is the fear of losing out. Queuing an hour early for a buffet is kiasu, and so is buying three umbrellas "in case".

**Syok** means fantastic or satisfying. A good meal is syok, and so is the air-conditioning after a walk at noon.

**Kantoi** means caught out, and people usually say it with a grin.

**Teruk** means terrible. It's mostly used about traffic.

**Cincai** means whatever, or done any old how: "Just cincai put there."

**Jom** means let's go. "Jom lepak" means let's go and hang out.

**Lepak** is hanging out with no plan in particular. Mamak restaurants were made for it.

**Belanja** means to treat someone. When a colleague says "Today I belanja", lunch is on them, and the next one is on you.

**Balik kampung** means going back to your home town. You'll hear it every festive season, just before the city's highways empty out.

## A few Malay words that make life easier

Manglish borrows a lot from Bahasa Malaysia, and these few words will get you through most market stalls and taxi rides:

| **Sama-sama** | You're welcome |
| **Berapa?** | How much? |

| **Tak boleh** | Cannot |

## Go easy on the lah

Malaysians are delighted when a foreigner drops a "lah" in the right place, and they can tell at once when it's forced. Start with the handful you'll actually use: can, tapau, jom, kurang manis and terima kasih. The rest will come with time, and a year from now you may well catch yourself asking a friend back home to on the light.
