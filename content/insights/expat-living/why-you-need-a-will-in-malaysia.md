You planned the move to Malaysia carefully: the visa, the fixed deposit, the home, the school. Most residents never plan for what happens to those assets if they are no longer here to manage them.

If you hold a Premium Visa Programme (PVIP) or Malaysia My Second Home (MM2H) pass, you almost certainly own assets in Malaysia. Without a Malaysian will, your family may wait a long time, in a foreign legal system, to reach them. A properly drafted will is the simplest and cheapest way to prevent that.

This guide explains why, in plain language.

## "I already have a will back home"

A will made in your home country does not automatically unlock assets in Malaysia. Malaysian banks, land offices and the Road Transport Department act only on an order of the Malaysian High Court. Your executor must obtain one before anything can be released, sold or transferred.

How hard that is depends on where your home-country grant was issued.

{% table caption="What your executor must do in Malaysia, by where the grant of probate was issued" %}
| Where your grant of probate was issued | What your executor must do in Malaysia |
| --- | --- |
| **A Commonwealth country** (for example the UK, Singapore, Australia, India, Canada) | Apply to the Malaysian High Court to reseal the foreign grant under section 52 of the Probate and Administration Act 1959. Faster than starting again, but it can begin only after probate is complete at home. |
| **A non-Commonwealth country** (for example the US, Japan, South Korea, China, the UAE) | Resealing is not available. A fresh application for a grant must be made in Malaysia, usually with translated and authenticated foreign documents. |

Either way, your Malaysian assets sit in a queue behind your home-country estate. A separate Malaysian will, covering only your Malaysian assets, lets your executor apply to the Malaysian court directly and in parallel.

There is a second reason. Under Malaysian conflict-of-law rules, real estate in Malaysia follows Malaysian law, while movable assets such as bank deposits generally follow the law of your domicile. A will written for another legal system may not deal cleanly with a Malaysian condominium or a Malaysian bank's requirements.

## What happens if you have no will at all

Dying without a will is called dying intestate, and it is the slowest and most expensive route for your family. Four things follow.

1. **Nobody has authority to act.** Until the High Court issues letters of administration, no one can legally deal with your assets. Accounts in your sole name stay frozen.
2. **Your family must find sureties.** An administrator is normally required to provide an administration bond, backed by two sureties whose assets match the value of the estate. For a foreign family with few local connections this is a real obstacle. An executor named in a will does not need one.
3. **The law decides who inherits.** For non-Muslims, the Distribution Act 1958 fixes the shares for assets it governs, including your Malaysian real estate. Your wishes are not considered.
4. **Foreign law may have to be proven.** Because movable assets generally follow the law of your domicile, the court may need evidence of your home country's inheritance rules. That means more time and cost.

The fixed shares under the Distribution Act 1958 surprise many people.

{% table caption="Intestacy shares for non-Muslims under the Distribution Act 1958" %}
| You are survived by | Spouse | Children | Parents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Spouse only** | Whole estate | — | — |
| **Spouse and children** | 1/3 | 2/3 | — |
| **Spouse and parents** | 1/2 | — | 1/2 |
| **Spouse, children and parents** | 1/4 | 1/2 | 1/4 |

A spouse with children receives only one third. Parents take a share even if they are financially independent. Unmarried partners and stepchildren receive nothing, and so does any charity or friend you had in mind.

## What PVIP and MM2H holders have at stake

Residence programme participants hold more in Malaysia than they realise, and most of it is in one person's sole name.

{% table caption="Malaysian assets and why each gets stuck without a Malaysian grant" %}
| Asset | Why it gets stuck without a Malaysian grant |
| --- | --- |
| **Programme fixed deposit** | Held in the principal applicant's name. The bank will not release it to family without a court grant. |
| **Malaysian bank accounts** | Sole-name accounts are frozen once the bank is notified of the death. |
| **Residential property** | The land office will not register a transfer or sale without a grant naming the person entitled to act. |
| **Vehicles** | Ownership cannot be transferred or the car sold. |
| **Investments, shares in a Malaysian company, club memberships** | Each institution asks for the same document: the Malaysian grant. |

The fixed deposit matters most. It is often the largest liquid sum the family has in Malaysia, and the moment they need it is the moment it becomes unreachable. Meanwhile mortgage instalments, maintenance fees, school fees and living costs continue.

## Your spouse and children

A will protects the people who came to Malaysia because of you. Your spouse and children usually hold their passes as your dependants, so your death raises an immigration question and a financial one at the same time.

A will helps in three ways.

- **It names who is in charge.** Your spouse, or another person you trust, can apply to the court immediately as executor, with no sureties and no family negotiation over who should act.
- **It names a guardian.** If you have children under 18 in Malaysia, you can appoint a testamentary guardian under the Guardianship of Infants Act 1961. Without one, the question is left to the court if both parents are gone.
- **It keeps money flowing.** Your will can direct how funds are held and used for your children's schooling and upkeep until they reach an age you choose.

On the pass itself, MOTAC's guide says an MM2H pass is transferable to the next of kin among the registered dependants on the death of the principal (Silver p.36). It does not say how the fixed deposit is treated in that case, and we have not found a published equivalent for PVIP. Ask your licensed agent what applies to your programme, and get the answer in writing while it is still a planning question.

## If you are Muslim

The Wills Act 1959 and the Distribution Act 1958 do not apply to Muslims. In Malaysia, a Muslim's estate is distributed under Islamic inheritance law (faraid), and the equivalent of a will is a wasiat.

A wasiat generally lets you direct up to one third of your estate to people who are not your faraid heirs. It also lets you name an executor (wasi), which speeds up administration in the same way a conventional will does.

For Muslim participants from the Middle East, South Asia and elsewhere, the interaction between Malaysian Syariah law and your home country's rules needs specific advice. The planning need is the same, but the instrument is different.

## What a Malaysian will involves

Making a valid will in Malaysia is quick and inexpensive compared with the cost of not having one. Under the Wills Act 1959, a non-Muslim's will must meet these requirements.

- You are at least 18 and of sound mind.
- The will is in writing.
- You sign it in front of at least two witnesses, both present at the same time, who then sign in front of you.
- No witness is a beneficiary or the spouse of a beneficiary. A gift to a witness is void.

Foreigners can make a Malaysian will. Citizenship and residence status are not conditions.

A will drafted for a PVIP or MM2H holder should also do the following.

1. **Limit itself to Malaysian assets.** State clearly that it covers only your assets in Malaysia, so it sits alongside your home-country will.
2. **Not revoke your other wills.** A standard clause revoking "all previous wills" can cancel your home-country will by accident. Tell both lawyers about both documents.
3. **Name an executor who can act in Malaysia.** A local or regional executor, or a trust company, avoids delay. Name a substitute as well.
4. **List the key assets.** Fixed deposit, bank accounts, property, vehicles, company shares.
5. **Appoint a guardian** if you have minor children.
6. **Be kept where it can be found.** Tell your executor and your family where the original is.

Review the will when you buy or sell property, when your family changes, or when you renew or change programme. Marriage generally revokes an earlier will under the Wills Act, so a new one is needed after you marry.
